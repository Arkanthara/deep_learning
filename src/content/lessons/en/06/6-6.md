## Understand the problem

GPUs execute many numerical operations in parallel. Large matrix products and convolutions fit this pattern well. To use a GPU, the involved parameters and tensors must reside on the same device; computation then occurs where the data are stored.

## How it works

CPU–GPU transfers are costly: avoid unnecessary round trips. Tensor.to can return the same tensor or a copy depending on the requested device, while Module.to moves registered parameters and buffers. The handout also introduces data parallelism: split a batch, run replicas, then aggregate results and gradients.

$$
Y=XW^\top\quad\text{requires compatible devices for }X\text{ and }W
$$

## Develop the reasoning

Actual cost combines computation, transfers and synchronisation. GPU work may launch asynchronously, so timing only the Python call can underestimate duration. Benchmark with appropriate timing and synchronise at measurement boundaries. Activations and optimiser states contribute to memory use, not only weights. Data parallelism distributes examples but must synchronise gradient contributions to keep replicas consistent.

## Worked example

Choose device = torch.device("cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu"), then move the model, inputs and targets. Creating a tensor with x.new_zeros(...) preserves x’s dtype and device.

## Understanding check

How many bytes does a float32 tensor of shape (32,64,128,128) contain, excluding other allocations?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the worked answer</summary>

32×64×128×128×4 = 134,217,728 bytes, or 128 MiB. Gradients, saved activations and optimiser states can substantially increase total memory.

</details>
