## Understand the problem

To a computer, an image is just an array of numbers. Recognising a chair across changes in lighting, viewpoint and texture requires crossing a semantic gap: moving from raw signal to useful information. Instead of writing every rule, we choose a parametric model and fit its parameters to examples.

## How it works

Classical methods often separate handcrafted features from a learned predictor. A deep network learns a sequence of representations, jointly optimised end to end. The handout traces perceptrons, LeNet, AlexNet and Transformers. Their common foundation is a graph of tensor operators trained using the chain rule, stochastic descent and parallel computation.

$$
f_\theta(x)=f_L\circ f_{L-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_1(x)
$$

## Develop the reasoning

The model $f_\theta$ is a family of functions: its architecture determines possible computations and $\theta$ their adjustable coefficients. A loss converts output quality into a number. Training chooses coefficients; inference then applies the fixed function to a new input. The same architecture can therefore behave very differently after two training runs. “End to end” means the final loss can also change early transformations instead of fixing features once and for all.

## Worked example

A CIFAR10 image contains 3 × 32 × 32 = 3,072 values. A network can transform these into edges, then patterns, then ten class scores. Even the edge detectors need not be handcrafted.

## Understanding check

One system computes edges with a fixed filter and learns a classifier. Another learns filters and classifier together. Which learns its representations end to end?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the worked answer</summary>

The second: its final loss can change the filters. The first learns a predictor, but its fixed features receive no task-driven updates.

</details>
