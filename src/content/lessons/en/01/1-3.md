## Understand the problem

Filter and activation visualisations suggest that networks learn structures suited to the data. Early layers may respond to orientations and textures; later layers combine these responses. A visualisation provides evidence about the computation without fully explaining a decision.

## How it works

The handout juxtaposes visualisation, DeepDream and adversarial examples. Optimising an input to activate a neuron reveals patterns preferred by the model. Conversely, small perturbations chosen by optimisation can trigger an error. Connections with biological vision are interesting, but similarity does not make the systems equivalent.

$$
x^*=\arg\max_x f_k(x)
$$

## Develop the reasoning

Distinguish three questions. What does a unit compute on observed data? Inspect its activations. To which small variation is a score sensitive? Differentiate with respect to the input. What pattern maximises the response under constraints? Optimise the input. These procedures answer different questions and can produce different images without contradiction. A useful comparison keeps the same target class, documents preprocessing and checks multiple inputs rather than one convincing example.

## Worked example

Two images that look almost identical to a person can produce different classes. A directed perturbation exploits a sensitive model direction; it is not equivalent to random noise of the same magnitude.

## Understanding check

A unit responds strongly to five cat images. Can we conclude it exclusively represents cats?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the worked answer</summary>

No. Counterexamples and other categories must be tested: the response could track a texture, colour or context correlated with cats in those images.

</details>
