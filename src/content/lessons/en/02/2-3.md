## Understand the problem

Imagine repeating training on new datasets. Bias measures the gap between the mean prediction and the target; variance measures prediction spread across training runs. The course decomposes squared error around a fixed target into squared bias and variance.

## How it works

An overly simple family often introduces bias; a very flexible family can become sensitive to sample fluctuations. Regularisation trades some flexibility for stability. With a noisy observed target, irreducible noise must be added to the decomposition. The source also connects penalties with Bayesian priors.

$$
\mathbb E_{\mathcal D}[(\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x)-y)^2]=(\mathbb E_{\mathcal D}[\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x)]-y)^2+\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal D}(\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x))
$$

## Develop the reasoning

Write f̂−y = (f̂−E[f̂])+(E[f̂]−y), then expand the square. The expected cross term is zero because E[f̂−E[f̂]]=0. What remains is the first term’s variance and the second term’s squared bias. Expectations here are over possible training sets at fixed x. If Y=m(x)+ε with E[ε|x]=0, evaluating against Y adds Var(ε|x) under the usual independence assumptions. Reducing that component requires additional information, not simply a more flexible model.

## Worked example

If a fixed target is 3 and predictions are equally likely to be 2 or 4, bias is zero, variance is 1 and mean squared error is 1.

## Understanding check

An estimator always predicts 4 for a fixed target of 3. What are its bias, variance and squared error?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the worked answer</summary>

Bias is 1, variance is 0 and error is 1²+0=1. A perfectly stable estimator can still be systematically wrong.

</details>
