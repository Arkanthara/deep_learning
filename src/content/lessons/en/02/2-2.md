## Understand the problem

An underfit model captures too little structure; an overfit model follows training-set peculiarities, including noise. Capacity describes its representational richness. The course illustrates this with k-nearest neighbours and polynomial regression.

## How it works

A small k produces a highly flexible local boundary; a large k smooths it. For a polynomial, increasing degree increases fitting possibilities. Training error can therefore decrease while error on new data rises. Penalising coefficients can constrain flexibility without explicitly removing terms.

$$
\mathcal L(\alpha)=\sum_n\left(\sum_{d=0}^D\alpha_dx_n^d-y_n\right)^2+\lambda\|\alpha\|_2^2
$$

## Develop the reasoning

The handout’s candidate example exposes the mechanism: getting ten binary answers right by chance has probability 1/1024. Among 1,000 independent candidates, the probability that at least one succeeds is 1−(1−1/1024)¹⁰⁰⁰, about 62%. Selection turns an apparently rare individual result into a common event. Testing many models on the same observations can have the same effect. Regularisation and validation aim to control this process, not just decrease numerical error.

## Worked example

A degree-9 polynomial can interpolate ten points with distinct inputs. If the points contain noise, exact interpolation may create large oscillations between them.

## Understanding check

Why can increasing k in k-NN reduce overfitting while increasing bias?
