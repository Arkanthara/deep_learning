## Understand the problem

A loss measures error on one example. Risk is its expectation over the real population, which is usually unknown. To learn, we replace that expectation with an average over available examples: empirical risk. The model is selected to make this average small.

## How it works

For a function fixed independently of the data, the empirical average is an unbiased risk estimator under i.i.d. sampling. After model selection on those same data, this guarantee no longer applies directly. The chosen function now depends on the sample: this is the central generalisation challenge.

$$
R(f)=\mathbb{E}[\ell(f,Z)],\qquad\hat R(f)=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\ell(f,z_n)
$$

## Develop the reasoning

Z may denote a pair (X,Y) in supervised learning or just X in density estimation. For a fixed function, linearity of expectation gives E[Σℓ(f,Zₙ)/N] = ΣE[ℓ(f,Zₙ)]/N = R(f). But minimising on the same data selects favourable fluctuations: E[min R̂] is not min E[R̂]. Thus distinguish the optimisation problem, reducing observed loss, from the statistical problem, predicting future performance.

## Worked example

Zero average error on ten examples alone says nothing about future examples. A predictor could memorise the ten answers and fail everywhere else.

## Understanding check

Two models have the same true risk. You choose the one with smaller error on a small sample. Is that selected error an independent estimate?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the worked answer</summary>

No. The choice exploits sample fluctuations. Evaluate the selected model on another independent sample for a measurement that did not guide selection.

</details>
