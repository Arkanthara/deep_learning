## Understand the problem

Pooling summarises a local region by a statistic, usually a maximum or mean. It often reduces spatial dimensions while preserving channel count. Unlike a standard convolution, it acts independently on each channel and learns no filter.

## How it works

Max pooling transmits the strongest value. During backpropagation, sensitivity returns to the selected maximum position. It provides tolerance to some local variations but loses positional information. Its default stride often equals the kernel size, unlike convolutions.

$$
y_{n,c,i,j}=\max_{(u,v)\in\mathcal W_{i,j}}x_{n,c,u,v}
$$

## Develop the reasoning

Max is piecewise affine: with a unique maximum, its derivative is 1 for the selected element and 0 for others. Ties require a subgradient convention. Average pooling distributes sensitivity uniformly across averaged values. Both reduce resolution differently: max retains a strong detection while averaging retains overall activity. Shifts crossing window boundaries can abruptly change outputs.

## Worked example

A block [1,3;2,0] gives 3 with max pooling and 1.5 with average pooling. A batch (8,16,32,32) becomes (8,16,16,16) with 2×2 blocks and stride 2.

## Understanding check

Max pooling over [1,4,2,0] receives output gradient 3. What gradient reaches the four inputs?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the worked answer</summary>

The unique maximum is 4, so the gradient is [0,3,0,0]. Average pooling over those four values would give [0.75,0.75,0.75,0.75].

</details>
