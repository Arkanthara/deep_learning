## Understand the problem

A denoising autoencoder receives a corrupted version x̃ and tries to recover x. It thereby learns dependencies between signal components. Masking, additive noise and reduced resolution define different restoration tasks, including denoising and super-resolution.

## How it works

With squared loss, the ideal optimal estimator is the conditional mean E[X|x̃]. If several sharp images are plausible, their average can be blurry and resemble none of them. Noise2Noise can learn without clean targets when target noise has zero conditional mean and is appropriately independent of input noise. Marginal unbiasedness alone is not sufficient in all cases.

$$
f^*(\tilde x)=\mathbb E[X\mid\tilde X=\tilde x]
$$

## Develop the reasoning

At fixed observation x̃, minimising E[(X−a)²|x̃] over a gives derivative 2(a−E[X|x̃]), hence a=E[X|x̃]. This is a property of the loss, not an optimisation failure. For noisy targets Y=X+δ, the same optimum is retained if E[δ|x̃]=0. If target noise depends on input noise, that condition can fail. Adversarial losses target other properties, notably realism, with a different tradeoff against exact fidelity.

## Worked example

If a corrupted observation is equally likely to correspond to x = −1 or x = +1, the squared-error estimator produces 0. Mean error is minimal even if 0 is never a real target.

## Understanding check

If the target is 0 with probability 0.75 and 4 with probability 0.25, which prediction minimises MSE?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the worked answer</summary>

The mean is 1. Expected loss there is 0.75×1²+0.25×3²=3, compared with 4 when always predicting 0.

</details>
