## Understand the problem

A convolution can be written as a large structured matrix A. Transposed convolution applies Aᵀ: each input value distributes contributions into an output region. This operation naturally appears in the derivative of convolution with respect to its input and enables decoders that increase resolution.

## How it works

Transposing does not mean inverting: AᵀA is generally not the identity, especially if convolution discarded information. Transposed-convolution stride spaces contributions in the output. Uneven overlap can create checkerboard artefacts. An alternative is to interpolate to a larger resolution and then apply ordinary convolution.

$$
H_{out}=(H-1)s-2p+d(k-1)+o+1
$$

## Develop the reasoning

Consider a padding-free 1D convolution: y₀=ax₀+bx₁ and y₁=ax₁+bx₂. Its matrix A has rows [a,b,0] and [0,a,b]. Applying Aᵀ to (u₀,u₁) gives (au₀, bu₀+au₁, bu₁). Contributions overlap at the centre, illustrating the transpose’s additive distribution. The initial convolution maps three values to two and generally cannot retain all information; simply transposing cannot undo that loss.

## Worked example

For input size 4, kernel 3, stride 2, padding 1, dilation 1 and output_padding 0, the output is (4−1)×2−2+2+1 = 7. output_padding resolves a size ambiguity; it does not restore lost values.

With a=1, b=2 and u=(3,4), compute the example’s transposed output.
