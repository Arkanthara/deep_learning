## Understand the problem

Wasserstein distance measures the minimum cost of transporting probability mass from one distribution to another. Unlike some divergences, it directly accounts for distance between locations. This can provide a smoother signal when real and generated distributions are separated.

## How it works

The dual formulation optimises the mean score difference of a 1-Lipschitz critic. It produces a real score, not a real/fake class probability. The Lipschitz constraint is essential: without it, scaling scores could increase the objective arbitrarily. The source presents weight clipping and then spectral normalisation, controlling linear operator norms. Clipping can overly restrict critic capacity.

$$
W_1(P,Q)=\sup_{\|f\|_{Lip}\le1}\left(\mathbb E_P[f(X)]-\mathbb E_Q[f(X)]\right)
$$

## Develop the reasoning

A 1-Lipschitz function satisfies |f(x)−f(y)|≤||x−y||: its score cannot vary faster than distance. For a linear layer in Euclidean norm, the constant is the weight’s largest singular value. Spectral normalisation estimates it, notably via power iteration, and rescales the weight. Constants multiply along a chain but may add across summed branches. The Wasserstein interpretation therefore requires attention to architecture and critic optimisation quality.

## Worked example

For point masses at 0 and 3, Wasserstein-1 distance is 3. Moving generated mass from 3 to 2 reduces the cost to 2 even though the distributions still do not overlap.

## Understanding check

A 1-Lipschitz critic gives f(0)=2. Which f(3) values are compatible in one dimension?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the worked answer</summary>

We require |f(3)−2|≤3, so f(3) lies in [−1,5]. Scores can be negative or exceed 1: they are not probabilities.

</details>
