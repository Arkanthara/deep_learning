## Understand the problem

A GAN pits a generator G, which transforms latent noise into samples, against a discriminator D, which distinguishes real and fake examples. The generator does not compare each output with a corresponding target: it learns through the discriminator’s judgement.

## How it works

For fixed G, the optimal discriminator is p_data/(p_data+p_G). In the ideal theoretical setting, the minimax objective connects to Jensen–Shannon divergence. In practice, updates alternate between D and G. During D’s step, fake samples are detached from G’s graph; during G’s step, gradients must pass through D into G. The nonsaturating generator loss −log D(G(z)) often gives a more useful early signal than log(1−D(G(z))).

$$
\min_G\max_D\;\mathbb E_{x\sim p_{data}}\log D(x)+\mathbb E_z\log(1-D(G(z)))
$$

## Develop the reasoning

At each x, the discriminator objective contains $p_{data}(x)\log D(x)+p_G(x)\log(1-D(x))$. Setting its D derivative to zero gives the density ratio. If distributions match, optimal D is 1/2 wherever density is positive: origin becomes indistinguishable. This assumes a sufficiently powerful discriminator and ideal optimisation. In practice G and D evolve together; their losses belong to a game rather than one shared decreasing function. Low D loss may signal poor G performance, not good overall training.

## Worked example

If the generator produces only one very convincing digit, it may fool D while ignoring nine classes. This is a form of mode collapse: realism and diversity must be examined together.

## Understanding check

If p_data(x)=3p_G(x)>0, what is the optimal discriminator there?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the worked answer</summary>

D*(x)=3/(3+1)=0.75. This compares local densities; it does not mean the whole generator is 75% correct.
