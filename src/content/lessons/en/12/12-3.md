## Understand the problem

A word can be identified by an integer, but that integer does not define semantic distance. An embedding table maps each ID to a learned vector. In CBOW, context predicts a central word; skip-gram learns to predict context items from the word.

## How it works

Normalisation over a large vocabulary can be expensive. Negative sampling replaces full classification with discrimination of observed pairs and sampled negative pairs. nn.Embedding efficiently selects rows without constructing one-hot vectors. Recurrent sequence-to-sequence models encode a sentence and decode its translation; beam search retains multiple candidate prefixes. BLEU measures n-gram overlap with reference translations without perfectly measuring meaning.

$$
\mathrm{cosine}(u,v)=\frac{u^\top v}{\|u\|_2\|v\|_2}
$$

## Develop the reasoning

Multiplying a one-hot eₖ by table E selects exactly row k; lookup avoids constructing all the zeros. During backward only used rows receive contributions, added if a word appears multiple times. Learned spaces reflect corpus distributions, including undesirable associations. In translation, the decoder maximises a conditional sequence probability. Greedily choosing the best next word need not give the best entire sentence, motivating exploration of multiple prefixes.

## Worked example

A table of 10,000 words in dimension 128 has 1,280,000 parameters. IDs of shape (B,T) produce a tensor (B,T,128). Cosine similarity compares vector directions, not their integer IDs.

## Understanding check

Why do two occurrences of the same ID update one embedding rather than independent vectors?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the worked answer</summary>

They select the same table row and therefore the same shared parameter. Gradients from both uses are added.

</details>
