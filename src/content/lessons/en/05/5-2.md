## Understand the problem

Computing the full dataset gradient can be expensive before making even one update. SGD estimates it using one example or, in practice, a mini-batch. Appropriate sampling gives a noisy estimate of the mean gradient but enables more frequent updates.

## How it works

Momentum retains an average of past directions to reduce some oscillations. Adam estimates first and second moments and adapts update scale per coordinate. Bias corrections compensate for initialising these moments at zero. No optimiser removes the need to tune the learning rate.

$$
g_t=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B\nabla_\theta\ell_{n_b}(\theta_t),\quad v_t=\beta v_{t-1}+g_t,\quad\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta v_t
$$

## Develop the reasoning

If batch indices are sampled uniformly, the expected mean gradient equals the training-set mean gradient. Under independence its variance decreases as 1/B, but a large batch costs more computation. Adam tracks $m_t=\beta_1m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)g_t$ and $v_t=\beta_2v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)g_t^2$, then uses versions corrected by $1-\beta_i^t$. Dividing by $\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\epsilon$ adapts scale; it does not exactly invert the Hessian.

## Worked example

For 1,000 examples and batches of 100, an epoch performs ten mini-batch updates, compared with one update for full-dataset descent.

## Understanding check

Why can shuffling help when data are ordered by class?

<details>
<summary>Reveal the worked answer</summary>

Successive homogeneous batches would steer updates towards different classes. Shuffling makes batches more representative while respecting sequence or group constraints where relevant.
